A Visual Primer

How do we measure progress in organizational governance?

Researchers, journalists, and policymakers increasingly need to assess what AI developers, platforms, and other organizations are doing — and whether they are improving over time. The choice of measurement method is rarely neutral: it shapes what gets counted as progress, what gets ignored, and how comparable the results are across organizations and across years.

§ 01

A spectrum from strict comparability to narrative depth

Most assessment methods sit on a continuum. At one end, binary criteria produce highly comparable scores that strip away context. At the other, narrative case studies preserve rich context at the cost of standardization. Numerical rubrics, tiered maturity models, and quantitative metrics occupy the middle.

§ 02

Eight methods in practice

The three approaches commonly used in AI governance research — binary criteria, numerical rubric scoring, and qualitative discussion — anchor a broader family of methods. The remaining five (maturity tiers, quantitative metrics, third-party audit, benchmarking, and comparative ranking) are increasingly being borrowed from adjacent fields: corporate governance, human-rights reporting, cybersecurity, and ESG.

01

Binary evaluation against set criteria

Yes / No · met / not met · pass / fail

Each criterion is a single, atomic question with a binary answer. Evaluators decide whether the organization meets a defined condition — for instance, "publishes a model card for each frontier release" — and report a tally across many such criteria. Aggregate scores are usually percentages.

Example in the wild The Foundation Model Transparency Index (FMTI) scores companies on roughly one hundred binary indicators. See e.g. AI21 Labs' December 2025 disclosure: stanford-crfm/fmti — AI21_Labs_fmtransparencyreport_Dec2025.csv.
Strengths
  • Maximum comparability across organizations and over time
  • Low inter-rater disagreement when criteria are sharply defined
  • Cheap to apply once the criteria set is built
  • Easy to communicate to non-specialist audiences
Limitations
  • Loses all gradation — a token gesture and a thorough programme score the same
  • Encourages "tick-box" compliance; companies optimise for the checklist
  • Whether a criterion is "met" can hinge on edge-case judgements that are hidden in the binary
  • Heavily dependent on the quality of the criteria set
02

Scoring against a categorical rubric

Letter grades · ordinal categories · simple scales

A small ordinal scale — often three to five categories such as "no action / partial / full" or letter grades — applied to each criterion. Each level is given a short descriptor. Sits between binary and detailed numerical scoring: it preserves enough gradation to distinguish weak from strong performers without requiring elaborate calibration.

Example in the wild The Brennan Center's Tech Companies Pledged to Protect Elections from AI — Here's How They Did evaluates platform commitments using a small set of qualitative ratings. See: brennancenter.org — Tech Companies Pledged to Protect Elections.
Strengths
  • Captures meaningful gradation without the calibration cost of long scales
  • Categories map naturally onto public narratives ("most failed", "two improved")
  • Easier to apply consistently than a 0–10 scale
Limitations
  • Boundary cases between adjacent categories can be contentious
  • Aggregating ordinal categories into numerical scores is methodologically fraught
  • Short descriptors leave substantial room for evaluator drift
03

Scoring against a detailed numerical rubric

Calibrated scales · 0–9 · 1–7 · anchored levels

A longer scale — typically six to ten points — where each numerical level is anchored to a specific descriptor that defines what qualifies as that score. The descriptors do most of the work: they convert what looks like a subjective judgement into a near-procedural classification. Strong rubrics also distinguish what they are not measuring.

Example in the wild A research-grade rubric defining each integer score (e.g. an internal 0–9 rubric scoring evidence of AI scheming-related behaviours, where 0 is "not relevant", 5 is "moderate signal — transcript-level evidence, mundane explanations less plausible", and 9 is "exceptional signal — verifiable, strategic, clearly covert"). Detailed anchor descriptors give evaluators concrete criteria for each level.
Strengths
  • Captures fine-grained variation while remaining quantitative
  • Anchored descriptors substantially improve inter-rater reliability
  • Makes the implicit reasoning behind a score auditable
  • Supports statistical analysis (medians, distributions, correlation)
Limitations
  • Building the rubric is itself a substantial research project
  • Long scales tempt false precision — a 6 versus a 7 may not be meaningful
  • Numerical mean of ordinal anchors should be interpreted with care
  • Time-intensive to apply at scale
04

Qualitative discussion of how a criterion was met

Narrative · case description · process documentation

Rather than scoring, the evaluator (or the organization itself) describes what was done, how it was done, and what evidence supports the account. Outputs are prose: incident write-ups, programme summaries, before-and-after descriptions. Particularly common in commitments-based regimes — voluntary pledges where the action taken is heterogeneous across signatories.

Example in the wild Thorn's Safety by Design — Three Month Progress Report describes, in narrative form, each commitment and the concrete steps taken by signatory companies to deliver on them: info.thorn.org — SafetyByDesign Three Month Progress Report.
Strengths
  • Preserves context, causation, and the texture of organizational action
  • Allows partial credit and acknowledges trade-offs
  • Surfaces methodology, not just outcome — useful for replication
  • Tolerates heterogeneity between organizations
Limitations
  • Hard to compare across organizations or against a baseline
  • Prose can obscure as much as it reveals — strong writing ≠ strong action
  • Self-reported narratives have obvious selection bias
  • Difficult to aggregate or summarize
05

Tiered maturity model

Levels of maturity · "ad-hoc → managed → optimized"

Each area of governance is assigned a developmental level (often four or five), where levels are nested: a higher level presupposes the practices of all lower levels. Inherited from software process modelling (CMMI) and now common in cybersecurity and AI governance frameworks. The model encodes a theory of progression — that some practices are foundational and others advanced.

Example in the wild NIST's AI Risk Management Framework profile work and ISO/IEC 42001 use tiered language for organizational AI management. Many internal corporate assessments adopt a 1–5 maturity scale: initial · repeatable · defined · managed · optimizing.
Strengths
  • Captures developmental trajectory rather than a static snapshot
  • Encodes a theory of which practices come before others
  • Provides clear "next step" guidance for the organization being assessed
  • Well-understood by corporate compliance audiences
Limitations
  • Assumes a single linear path — may not match real-world variation
  • Levels are aspirational; few organizations actually reach the top tier
  • Can reward process maturity over substantive outcomes
06

Quantitative metrics & key indicators

Counts · rates · ratios · response times

Hard numbers about real-world activity: number of models red-teamed before release, percentage of takedown requests actioned within 24 hours, hours of staff trained, number of external researchers given access. Rather than scoring against a criterion, the organization reports the underlying activity and the audience interprets.

Example in the wild Platform transparency reports (e.g. content moderation statistics under the EU Digital Services Act) and frontier-lab system cards that report the number and type of safety evaluations run. Many ESG frameworks follow the same logic.
Strengths
  • Verifiable and reproducible where the underlying data is well-defined
  • Supports trend analysis over time
  • Avoids evaluator subjectivity at the measurement step
Limitations
  • Goodhart's law — once a metric becomes a target, it stops measuring what it measured
  • Counts measure activity, not quality or outcome
  • Self-reported numbers can be defined inconsistently across organizations
  • Important governance work (judgement calls, culture, escalation paths) resists quantification
07

Third-party audit & certification

Independent verification · pass / fail certification

An external auditor, working against a published standard, examines the organization's documentation, processes, and (sometimes) systems, and issues a pass/fail certification or an audit report. The key difference from self-assessment is independence: the assessor is contractually accountable for the rigour of the assessment.

Example in the wild ISO/IEC 42001 (AI management systems) certifications; SOC 2 reports applied to AI infrastructure; emerging proposals for mandatory third-party audits of frontier models under the EU AI Act and several national frameworks.
Strengths
  • Independence increases credibility for external audiences
  • Auditor has professional liability for assessment quality
  • Standardized methodology allows comparability across firms
  • Familiar to investors, regulators, and procurement teams
Limitations
  • Expensive — both for the auditor and the audited
  • The auditing profession for AI is still nascent; methodology is unsettled
  • Audit market dynamics (clients pay auditors) can create independence concerns
  • Certifies process, not necessarily safety outcomes
08

Benchmark & standardized evaluation performance

Standardised tests · capability evals · safety benchmarks

The organization (or a third party) runs a fixed, public evaluation against the system or process being assessed. Performance on the benchmark stands in for performance on the underlying capability. Distinctive from rubric scoring because what is being assessed is an output behaviour, not a governance practice. Useful when the question is "does the system behave well" rather than "is the organization governing well".

Example in the wild HELM, MLCommons AI Safety benchmarks, and bespoke evaluation suites such as MASK or SimpleSafetyTests. Frontier labs increasingly publish benchmark performance in system cards.
Strengths
  • Highly reproducible — anyone can re-run the benchmark
  • Directly measures system behaviour rather than organizational claims
  • Allows clean comparison between systems
Limitations
  • Benchmarks become saturated or memorized — last year's hard test is today's training data
  • Measures only what is testable; misses governance, culture, escalation
  • Strong incentive to teach to the test
  • Benchmark performance ≠ deployment behaviour
09

Comparative ranking & leaderboards

League tables · composite indices · relative position

A composite index built on top of one of the methods above, surfaced as a ranking of organizations. The unit of progress is relative position: did Company A move up four places, did Company B drop. Often the most consequential layer publicly, because the press picks up the leaderboard even when the underlying methodology is more nuanced.

Example in the wild FMTI's overall company rankings; Ranking Digital Rights; AI Index leaderboards. Each layers a public ranking on top of an underlying scoring methodology.
Strengths
  • Highly legible to non-specialist audiences
  • Creates a competitive dynamic that can accelerate adoption of good practices
  • A single number is easier to track over time than a portfolio of indicators
Limitations
  • Rankings hide absolute progress — everyone can improve while ranks stay flat
  • Weighting choices in the composite are often arbitrary but consequential
  • Encourages organizations to engage with the index rather than the underlying problem
  • Movement up and down can be statistical noise

§ 05

Three cross-cutting considerations

Beyond the choice of method, three design decisions shape the integrity of any assessment, regardless of which method is used.

— I —

Who is doing the assessing?

Self-assessment (the organization scores itself), peer assessment, civil-society assessment, regulator assessment, or contracted third-party audit each carry different credibility weights. Any method above can be applied by any of these actors — the choice of actor is often more consequential than the choice of method.

— II —

Static snapshot or longitudinal?

A one-off score tells you where things stand; the same methodology repeated annually tells you whether they are improving. Longitudinal designs are far more useful for progress measurement specifically, but require methodological stability — every change to the criteria breaks the time series.

— III —

Process or outcome?

Most governance assessment measures process: does the policy exist, is the team in place, did the evaluation happen. Outcome measurement asks instead whether harm was prevented, whether systems behaved well in deployment. Outcome data is harder to obtain but speaks more directly to what the governance is supposed to achieve.

§ 06

Choosing a method

A rough decision aid. None of these mappings is strict — most strong research designs combine methods — but the questions below are the ones most worth asking before committing to an approach.

If you need to compare ten companies in a single article…
Binary criteria or categorical rubric. Both produce results that survive aggregation into a headline statistic. Pair with a methodology appendix that documents how each criterion was scored.
If you are tracking the same company over multiple years…
Any method, applied consistently. What matters most is methodological stability. Resist the urge to refine criteria between cycles; document changes carefully when you do.
If reviewers will challenge your evaluator judgements…
Detailed numerical rubric with anchor descriptors. The anchors make each score auditable. Pair with double-coding and inter-rater agreement statistics where stakes are high.
If the field is too new for stable criteria…
Qualitative narrative. Premature quantification of novel phenomena hides more than it reveals. Narrative case studies surface the categories that future rubrics will eventually score against.
If the audience is corporate or regulatory…
Maturity model or third-party audit. Both translate cleanly into corporate governance language and procurement processes.
If you want to influence behaviour, not just describe it…
Comparative ranking, built on a defensible underlying method. Public ranking creates competitive pressure. The risk is that organizations optimise for the index rather than the underlying problem — design the underlying method accordingly.
If the question is "is the system safe", not "is the organization governing"…
Benchmark performance, supplemented by red-teaming. Process-based methods will not catch a well-governed but poorly-behaved system. Benchmarks catch behaviour; narrative captures emergent failure modes that benchmarks miss.
