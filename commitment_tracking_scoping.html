Commitment Tracking Platform Scoping

Across four convenings — Bletchley Park (November 2023), Seoul (May 2024), Paris (February 2025), and New Delhi (February 2026) — the AI Summit Series has produced a growing stack of voluntary commitments from both governments and companies. New Delhi alone yielded 95 commitments, and we have identified 178 in total.

What the Series has not produced is any mechanism to judge whether those commitments are good, or to track whether anyone is keeping them, from one summit to the next. This is now the most consistent criticism of the Series, as voluntary commitments without measurement tend to drift toward the vague and the unverifiable.

Purpose of doc

To showcase what can be achieved for the Commitment Tracking Platform, and highlight some of the scoping considerations.

Section 01

Core outcomes

This highlights the need for work that pushes towards two core outcomes:

Outcome 1

PAI holds organizations to account for the progress of current voluntary commitments.

Outcome 2 Optional

PAI improves the design of future voluntary commitments.

Section 02

Developing a Public Accountability Tracker

What is the progress in delivering on current voluntary commitments?

Purpose

A public accountability tool that credits strong governance practices and policy responses, while surfacing unmet ones. This will focus on commitments related to PAI strategic themes.

Format

Something engaging — an interactive website? With the SDG dashboard as an initial template.

Content
  • What commitments are relevant to PAI's strategic themes?
  • Optional  What is the 'quality' of each commitment?
  • What is the progress of the organization / government in meeting this commitment?
  • What is the overall progress of this commitment?

Doing this will require the following high-level activities

  1. Creating the set of commitments to review, based on themes, verifiability, and overall resource.
  2. Defining measurement methodology.
    • Individual vs. collective: is it a rating for each org / country plus an aggregate rating, or just an aggregate rating?
    • Quality of implementation: do we rate companies who are doing more higher, or just have a pass / fail methodology?
    • Sub-measures: can we split up larger commitments into sub-measures or metrics, and track them?
    • Do we rate countries where dozens of them have signed up to a commitment?
  3. Calibrate the rating methodology with two or more reviewers (external ones?).
  4. Optional  Define the quality-measurement methodology.
  5. Collecting relevant public information with an agent and human searching.
  6. Optional  Verifying information with relevant organizations, collecting new information through engagement, and providing a right to reply to ratings.
  7. Finalizing ratings and presentation.

Section 03

What actually is a voluntary commitment?

Even a 'simple' commitment has a lot of decisions to track properly

Example 01 Easy to verify
"Publish — by the next AI Summit — statistical insights derived from anonymised, aggregated and taxonomized usage data, either directly or (where relevant) through contributions to international efforts."

Signatories India 2026 — Amazon, Anthropic, BharatGen, Cohere, Google, Gynani.ai, G42, Meta, Microsoft, Mistral AI, OpenAI, Sarvam AI, Socket Labs

  • Individual vs. collective: do we just release how well orgs are meeting this 'as a whole', and have it be less scientific?
  • Quality of implementation: do we rate someone who published the minimum info the same as a company that releases quarterly, detailed updates?
  • Sub-measures: can we split this up into smaller data points to share?
  • Other: how do we recognize a contribution to international efforts?

A collective commitment breaks the per-signatory model, so who is accountable for a shared deliverable?

Example 02 Hard to verify
"Developing the Trusted AI Commons: Provide Reference Based Assessment Resources: The Commons will support the hosting of evaluation methodologies, instruments, and benchmarks that serve as non-prescriptive reference resources to guide consistent, comparable, and robust assessment of AI systems across jurisdictions."

Signatories India 2026 — Brazil, Cambodia, Canada, Colombia, Egypt, Ethiopia, France, Iceland, Italy, Japan, Mauritius, Mexico, Mozambique, Nigeria, Norway, Philippines, Serbia, Singapore, Switzerland, Tanzania, UK, UNICEF

  • How do we evaluate how well the Commons is being done?
  • How do we evaluate if each country is participating in it?
  • Do we evaluate Tanzania the same as the UK? Seems unfair.

Some commitments are too vague to assess at all, which is itself a finding

Example 03 Not verifiable
"We will keep addressing the risks of AI to information integrity and continue work on AI transparency."

Signatories Paris 2025 — 50+ countries

  • What's the baseline for this? What does 'addressing' mean?
  • Should we just mention this, but not measure progress?
Note

I think this is why assessing quality to improve future commitments is an important part of this work.

Section 04

Some initial analysis

I've looked at how easy the commitments are to track, and whether they are related to our three strategic themes. Of the 178 commitments:

  • 40are related to our three themes and trackable — but with a lot of work (i.e. not easy to do).
  • 11of those 40 are easier to track.
  • 4of those 11 are industry commitments closely related to our themes (six are closely related; four of those are industry, primarily from 2024).

The 178 commitments narrow in three steps: 178 in total, 40 related to our themes and trackable, 11 of those easier to track, and 4 strict-core industry commitments.

In total

178

commitments across four summits

Everything pledged at Bletchley Park, Seoul, Paris and New Delhi — by governments and companies alike.

In current set Strict core (4) Excluded

A rigorous analysis of ~4–11 commitments would be more defensible but won't be interesting enough to draw attention. Widening to ~40 lets us market it better and have greater impact, but would need more resourcing — daily project work and expert reviewers — to actually do. We could then group by themes or summits, which would make for a more interactive website experience as well.

Reference points

Comparable efforts that landed ran 8 people (Stanford Transparency Index) to 12 (MIT Governance Mapping), and the SDG team far more; there are one- or two-person versions but they haven't made a big impact. And our previous Progress Reporting work really only looked at four commitments.

This means the minimal tier is low resource but low-impact, while the maximal tier is a larger effort with a fraction of the headcount bigger teams use. Agent-assisted review helps, but there's a risk we'll try to do too much.

Section 06

Risks

The two key risks I see

RiskMitigation
Conflict of interest / independence. We are publicly grading organizations that are our funders, and so we might be seen as being 'too soft'. Publish methodology and evidence up front; be transparent about our relationship to organizations; consider independent or advisory review of grades before publication.
Launched but not maintained. This is a tracker, so it will require ongoing tracking from one summit to the next. Lots of these initiatives have lapsed over time, so we'll need to be sure of resourcing. Scope to the tier that can be sustained, not just built; more than one person on the project to sustain it if resourcing changes.

Other risks

RiskMitigation
Incorrect assessment. We miss information and provide incorrect assessments, leading to reputational damage. Agent and human review; more than one person doing the review.
Unfair grading of low-capacity signatories. Collective commitments span uneven actors (UK and Japan vs. Mozambique and Cambodia). A single bar could penalize lower-capacity states. — to be added
